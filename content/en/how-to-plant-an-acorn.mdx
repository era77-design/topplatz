Planting an acorn is a rewarding long-term project that allows you to grow a magnificent oak tree from scratch. The process involves selecting viable acorns, preparing them for germination through stratification, and then planting them in a suitable location or pot. With a little patience and proper care, you can contribute to new tree growth and enjoy the beauty of an oak.

## What You Need

- Healthy acorns (from a local oak tree)
- Bowl of water
- Paper towels or sand/peat moss
- Sealable plastic bag or container
- Small pot (at least 6 inches deep, if starting indoors)
- Well-draining potting mix or garden soil
- Trowel or small shovel
- Watering can

## Steps

### Step 1: Collect Healthy Acorns

The first step is to gather mature, healthy acorns from under an oak tree, ideally in the fall. Look for acorns that have fallen naturally, have their caps attached (or nearby), and show no signs of holes, mold, or discoloration. Avoid any that appear shriveled or overly soft, as these are unlikely to be viable. Collect more than you think you need, as not all will germinate.

### Step 2: Perform a Float Test

Once you've collected your acorns, place them in a bowl of water. Healthy, viable acorns will sink to the bottom. Any acorns that float are likely hollow, damaged, or infested with weevils, and should be discarded. This simple test helps you select the best candidates for planting and increases your chances of success.

### Step 3: Stratify Your Acorns

Many oak species require a period of cold, moist dormancy, known as stratification, to break dormancy and germinate. To do this, place the viable acorns in a plastic bag with a moist medium like paper towels, sand, or peat moss. Seal the bag and store it in your refrigerator (not freezer) for 60 to 90 days. Check periodically to ensure the medium remains moist and there's no mold growth.

### Step 4: Prepare Your Planting Site or Pot

After stratification, your acorns are ready for planting. If planting directly outdoors, choose a site with good drainage and ample sunlight, keeping in mind the mature size of an oak. If starting indoors, select a pot at least 6 inches deep with drainage holes and fill it with a well-draining potting mix. Ensure the soil is moist but not waterlogged.

### Step 5: Plant the Acorn Correctly

Plant each acorn about 1 inch deep, laying it on its side. If planting in a pot, place one acorn per pot to give it ample space to grow. If planting directly in the ground, space them several feet apart to allow for future growth. Gently cover the acorn with soil and pat it down lightly. For more detailed guidance, see our guide on [How to Plant an Acorn Successfully: A Step-by-Step Guide](/en/how-to-plant-an-acorn-successfully).

### Step 6: Provide Initial Care and Protection

Immediately after planting, water the soil thoroughly. Maintain consistent moisture, but avoid overwatering which can lead to rot. If planting outdoors, consider protecting the planted acorn from squirrels, deer, and other pests using mesh cages or chicken wire. Indoors, place the pot in a location with bright, indirect light.

### Step 7: Transplant Seedlings (if potted)

If you started your acorn in a pot, it will eventually need to be transplanted to its permanent outdoor location. This is typically done when the seedling is 6-12 inches tall, usually after one growing season. Choose a cool, overcast day for transplanting to minimize shock, and ensure the new site is prepared with well-draining soil.


## Tips & Tricks

- Choose acorns from native oak species that are well-suited to your local climate for the best chance of success.
- Plant multiple acorns, as not all will germinate or survive, increasing your odds of growing an oak.
- Patience is key; growing an oak from an acorn is a long-term project that requires several years for significant growth.
- Protect young seedlings from browsing animals like deer and rodents using tree shelters or fencing.
- Ensure good drainage in both pots and outdoor planting sites to prevent root rot.

## Warnings

- ⚠️ Beware of squirrels and other rodents; they are notorious for digging up and eating newly planted acorns.
- ⚠️ Avoid overwatering, especially in pots, as this can lead to fungal diseases and rot, killing the developing embryo.
- ⚠️ Do not plant acorns too deep; planting more than 1-2 inches deep can inhibit germination.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the best time to plant an acorn?**

The best time to plant an acorn is typically in late winter or early spring after it has undergone its required cold stratification period. This mimics the natural cycle of acorns falling in the fall and germinating after winter.

**How deep should I plant an acorn?**

Acorns should be planted relatively shallow, usually about 1 inch deep. Lay the acorn on its side and cover it lightly with soil, ensuring it has enough contact with the soil while still being close enough to the surface for the shoot to emerge.

**How long does it take for an acorn to sprout?**

After proper stratification, an acorn can sprout anywhere from a few weeks to several months once planted. Germination time varies depending on the oak species, soil temperature, and moisture conditions.

**What kind of soil do acorns need?**

Acorns thrive in well-draining soil. If planting in a pot, use a good quality potting mix. For outdoor planting, ensure the site has loamy soil that drains well and is not compacted. Most oak species prefer slightly acidic to neutral soil.

**Can I plant acorns directly outside?**

Yes, you can plant acorns directly outside, provided they have been naturally stratified by winter conditions or artificially stratified beforehand. Ensure the outdoor site offers protection from pests and has suitable soil conditions.

**How do I protect my planted acorns from animals?**

To protect planted acorns from squirrels, birds, and other animals, you can use a mesh cage, chicken wire, or a plastic tree shelter placed over the planting site. Bury the edges of the protection slightly to prevent digging.

**How often should I water a newly planted acorn?**

Keep the soil consistently moist but never waterlogged. Check the soil moisture daily, especially during dry periods. Water thoroughly when the top inch of soil feels dry to the touch, and reduce frequency as the seedling establishes.
