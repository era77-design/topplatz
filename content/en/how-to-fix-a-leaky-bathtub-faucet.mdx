A leaky bathtub faucet is often caused by a worn-out washer or O-ring inside the valve assembly. Fixing it yourself is typically a straightforward process that can save you money and prevent water waste. This guide will walk you through the steps to silence that drip.

## What You Need

- Screwdriver set (Phillips and flathead)
- Adjustable wrench or basin wrench
- Pliers
- Utility knife or small flathead screwdriver (for prying)
- Replacement washers, O-rings, or a new cartridge (specific to your faucet model)
- Plumber's grease (silicone-based)
- Rag or towel
- Flashlight (optional)

## Steps

### Step 1: Turn Off the Water Supply

Locate the main water shut-off valve for your house, or the specific shut-off valves for the bathroom if available. Turn the water off to prevent flooding during the repair. Turn on the faucet briefly to drain any remaining water from the pipes.

> 💡 **Tip:** Ensure all water is off before proceeding. If you're unsure, turn on another faucet in the house to confirm the main supply is indeed off.

### Step 2: Remove the Faucet Handle

Most faucet handles are secured by a screw, often hidden under a decorative cap. Carefully pry off the cap with a utility knife or small flathead screwdriver. Use the appropriate screwdriver to remove the screw, then pull the handle straight off.

> 💡 **Tip:** If the handle is stuck, try gently wiggling it while pulling, or apply a penetrating lubricant if it's metal (and clean thoroughly afterward).

### Step 3: Access the Faucet Stem/Cartridge

Once the handle is off, you'll likely see a decorative escutcheon or trim piece. This might unscrew by hand or require an adjustable wrench. Beneath it, you'll find the valve stem or cartridge, which controls water flow.

> 💡 **Tip:** Take a photo of the assembly before disassembling further to aid in reassembly.

### Step 4: Remove the Valve Stem or Cartridge

Depending on your faucet type (compression, cartridge, ball, or ceramic disc), you'll need to remove the stem or cartridge. For compression faucets, use a wrench to unscrew the packing nut, then pull out the stem. For cartridge faucets, there may be a retaining clip to remove before pulling out the cartridge.

> 💡 **Tip:** Note the orientation of the cartridge or stem as you remove it.

### Step 5: Replace Worn Components

Examine the removed stem or cartridge for worn-out rubber washers, O-rings, or seals. These are the most common culprits for leaks. Replace them with new ones that match the old parts. If your faucet uses a cartridge, it's often easiest to replace the entire cartridge unit. Apply a thin layer of plumber's grease to new O-rings for a better seal. If the components are severely corroded or the faucet is very old, you might consider a full replacement. For guidance on this, see [How to Change a Bathtub Faucet - A DIY Guide](/en/how-to-change-a-bathtub-faucet).

### Step 6: Reassemble the Faucet

Carefully reverse the disassembly steps. Insert the new or repaired stem/cartridge, secure any clips or packing nuts, replace the escutcheon, and finally reattach the handle. Ensure all connections are snug but avoid overtightening, which can damage components.

### Step 7: Restore Water and Test

Slowly turn the main water supply back on. Check for any immediate leaks around the faucet base. Turn the bathtub faucet on and off several times, testing both hot and cold water, to ensure the leak is gone and the faucet operates smoothly.


## Tips & Tricks

- Always identify your faucet brand and model if possible before buying replacement parts. This ensures you get the correct size and type of washer, O-ring, or cartridge.
- Keep a small container or stopper in the drain while working to prevent small screws or parts from falling down.
- If you encounter significant corrosion or difficulty removing parts, consider contacting a professional plumber to avoid further damage.

## Warnings

- ⚠️ Always shut off the water supply completely before starting any plumbing work to prevent flooding.
- ⚠️ Do not overtighten nuts or screws, as this can strip threads or crack plastic components.
- ⚠️ Be careful when prying off decorative caps or handles to avoid scratching the faucet finish.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

To fix a leaky bathtub faucet, you typically need to shut off the water, disassemble the faucet handle and housing, and replace worn internal components like rubber washers, O-rings, or the entire cartridge. Reassembly and testing will complete the repair.

**What causes a bathtub faucet to leak?**

The most common cause of a leaky bathtub faucet is worn-out internal rubber components such as washers, O-rings, or seals within the valve stem or cartridge. Over time, these parts degrade, leading to drips and leaks.

**What tools do I need to fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

You'll generally need a screwdriver set, an adjustable wrench or basin wrench, pliers, a utility knife, and the specific replacement parts (washers, O-rings, or a cartridge) for your faucet model. Plumber's grease is also recommended.

**Can I fix a leaky bathtub faucet myself, or should I call a plumber?**

Many leaky bathtub faucets can be fixed by a DIY enthusiast with basic tools and knowledge. However, if you're uncomfortable with plumbing, encounter significant corrosion, or can't identify the correct replacement parts, it's best to call a professional plumber.

**How do I fix bathtub faucet drips after turning it off?**

Drips after turning off the faucet are usually due to a faulty washer, O-ring, or cartridge that isn't sealing properly. Follow the steps in this guide to replace these worn components, which should resolve the continuous dripping.

**How long does it take to fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

For an experienced DIYer, fixing a leaky bathtub faucet can take as little as 15-30 minutes. If you're new to plumbing or encounter unexpected issues like stubborn parts, it might take an hour or more.

**What if I can't find the exact replacement parts for my faucet?**

If you can't find exact replacement parts, take the old components (washers, O-rings, or the entire cartridge) to a local hardware store or plumbing supply shop. They can often help you match them or suggest a universal alternative. If this isn't possible, consider replacing the entire faucet.
