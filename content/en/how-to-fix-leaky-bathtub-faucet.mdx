A leaky bathtub faucet is often caused by worn-out washers or O-rings, leading to annoying drips and wasted water. Fixing it yourself is a straightforward DIY task that can save you money and prevent further plumbing issues. This guide will walk you through the simple steps to stop the drip and restore your faucet.

## What You Need

- Screwdriver (Phillips and flathead)
- Adjustable wrench
- Pliers
- Utility knife
- Replacement washers/O-rings (specific to your faucet type)
- Plumber's grease (silicone-based)
- Clean rags/towels
- Flashlight (optional)

## Steps

### Step 1: Shut Off the Water Supply

Locate the main water shut-off valve for your home or the specific valve for your bathroom. Turn it clockwise until the water flow is completely stopped. This is a critical safety step to prevent flooding during the repair process and allows you to work without water pressure.

### Step 2: Drain the Faucet and Prepare the Area

Open the leaky faucet to drain any remaining water from the pipes, relieving pressure. Place a rag or towel over the drain opening to prevent small parts from falling in and getting lost. Use a clean towel to protect the tub surface from scratches while you work on the faucet.

### Step 3: Remove the Faucet Handle

Most faucet handles have a decorative cap that can be carefully pried off with a utility knife or flathead screwdriver to reveal a screw. Unscrew this screw, typically a Phillips head, and carefully pull the handle straight off. Some handles may simply unscrew without a visible cap.

### Step 4: Access and Remove the Faucet Stem/Cartridge

Underneath the handle, you'll likely find a decorative escutcheon or trim piece, which may also need to be unscrewed or pulled off. Use an adjustable wrench to carefully loosen and remove the packing nut, then pull out the faucet stem or cartridge. Note its orientation for proper reinstallation later.

### Step 5: Inspect and Replace Worn Components

Examine the rubber washers, O-rings, and springs on the stem or cartridge. These are the most common culprits for leaks. Replace any components that appear worn, cracked, or hardened with new ones of the exact same size and type. Apply a thin layer of plumber's grease to new O-rings for a better seal.

### Step 6: Reassemble the Faucet

Carefully reinsert the repaired or new stem/cartridge into the faucet body, ensuring it's properly seated in its original orientation. Tighten the packing nut with your adjustable wrench, being careful not to overtighten and strip the threads. Reattach any escutcheon, the handle, and its securing screw.

### Step 7: Restore Water Supply and Test for Leaks

Slowly turn the main water supply back on, watching for any immediate leaks around the faucet. Open the faucet to test the flow and check for drips. If a leak persists, you may need to tighten components slightly or re-examine the washers. If the problem continues, you might consider a full replacement, and you can find guidance on this in our [How to Change a Bathtub Faucet - DIY Guide](/en/how-to-change-bathtub-faucet).


## Tips & Tricks

- Before purchasing replacement parts, take photos of your faucet and the old components, or bring the old parts to the hardware store to ensure you get exact matches.
- Use plumber's grease on new O-rings and threads to help create a better seal and make future repairs easier.
- Avoid overtightening screws and nuts, as this can damage components or strip threads, leading to further leaks or breakage.
- Clean any mineral deposits or grime from the faucet's internal parts while it's disassembled to prevent future issues.

## Warnings

- ⚠️ Always turn off the main water supply before attempting any plumbing repairs to prevent flooding.
- ⚠️ Do not force stubborn parts; use penetrating oil if necessary, but avoid excessive force that could break the faucet.
- ⚠️ If you are unsure at any step or the leak persists after your repair attempts, consider calling a professional plumber.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How to fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

To fix a leaky bathtub faucet, you typically need to replace worn-out internal components like washers or O-rings. Start by shutting off the water, disassembling the handle and stem, replacing the faulty parts, and then reassembling everything carefully to restore a watertight seal.

**How to fix bathtub faucet?**

Fixing a bathtub faucet usually involves identifying the source of the leak, which is often a deteriorated washer or cartridge inside the faucet mechanism. The process includes turning off the water supply, carefully dismantling the faucet, replacing the necessary internal seals, and then putting it back together.

**What usually causes a bathtub faucet to leak?**

The most common cause of a leaky bathtub faucet is worn-out rubber washers, O-rings, or a faulty cartridge inside the faucet stem. Over time, these components harden, crack, or lose their elasticity, failing to create a watertight seal and allowing water to drip.

**What tools do I need to fix a leaky faucet?**

Essential tools for fixing a leaky faucet include a screwdriver (Phillips and flathead), an adjustable wrench, and pliers. You'll also need replacement washers, O-rings, or a new cartridge specific to your faucet model, along with plumber's grease.

**Can I fix a leaky faucet without calling a plumber?**

Yes, fixing a leaky bathtub faucet is a common DIY task that most homeowners can accomplish. By following a step-by-step guide and having the right replacement parts, you can often resolve the issue yourself without professional help, saving money and time.

**How long does it take to fix a leaky faucet?**

For a straightforward repair involving replacing washers or O-rings, fixing a leaky faucet typically takes about 30 minutes to an hour. The time can vary depending on your experience, the complexity of your faucet, and how easily the components can be accessed and disassembled.

**What should I do if my faucet still leaks after replacing parts?**

If your faucet continues to leak after replacing the washers or O-rings, recheck that all components are correctly seated and tightened. The issue might be a damaged valve seat, a faulty cartridge, or corrosion in the faucet body, which may require further inspection or a full faucet replacement.
