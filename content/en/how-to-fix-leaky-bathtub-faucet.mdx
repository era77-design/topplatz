A leaky bathtub faucet can be an irritating nuisance and a waste of water. Most common leaks are caused by worn-out washers, O-rings, or cartridges, which are relatively simple to replace. This guide will walk you through the steps to quickly diagnose and fix the drip, restoring peace and quiet to your bathroom.

## What You Need

- Screwdriver set (Phillips and flathead)
- Adjustable wrench
- Pliers
- Utility knife or small flathead screwdriver (for decorative caps)
- Replacement washers, O-rings, or cartridge (match existing parts)
- Plumber's grease (silicone-based)
- Rag or towel
- Wire brush (optional, for cleaning mineral deposits)
- Penetrating oil (optional, for stuck screws)

## Steps

### Step 1: Turn Off Water Supply

Before starting any plumbing work, it's crucial to shut off the water supply to your bathtub. Locate the main shut-off valve for your house, or if available, the specific shut-off valves for the bathroom or bathtub. Once turned off, run the faucet briefly to drain any remaining water from the pipes and relieve pressure.

### Step 2: Remove Faucet Handles

Most faucet handles are secured by a screw hidden under a decorative cap. Carefully pry off any caps with a utility knife or small flathead screwdriver. Use the appropriate screwdriver to remove the screws holding the handles in place, then gently pull the handles off the valve stems. Some handles may just pull straight off without a screw.

### Step 3: Remove Trim and Valve Stem/Cartridge

Once the handles are off, you'll likely see a escutcheon or trim plate. Remove any screws holding it and pull it away from the wall. This will expose the valve stems or cartridge. Use an adjustable wrench to carefully unscrew the packing nuts or retaining clips that hold the valve stems or cartridge in place. Gently pull out the valve stems or cartridge from the faucet body.

### Step 4: Inspect and Replace Worn Parts

Examine the removed valve stems or cartridge for signs of wear, such as cracked rubber washers, brittle O-rings, or corroded cartridge components. Take the worn parts to a hardware store to find exact replacements. Apply a thin layer of plumber's grease to new O-rings and washers before installing them onto the valve stems or into the cartridge. If replacing a cartridge, ensure the new one matches the old one perfectly.

### Step 5: Reassemble the Faucet

Carefully reinsert the repaired valve stems or the new cartridge into the faucet body, ensuring they are correctly oriented. Hand-tighten the packing nuts or secure the retaining clips, then use your adjustable wrench to snug them up without overtightening. Replace the escutcheon or trim plate, followed by the faucet handles and their decorative caps. Ensure all parts fit securely.

### Step 6: Restore Water and Test

Slowly turn the main water supply back on. Once the water is fully restored, carefully open the bathtub faucet to check for leaks. Look closely around the handles and spout for any drips. If the leak persists, you may need to re-tighten components or re-evaluate the replacement parts. Enjoy your now drip-free bathtub!


## Tips & Tricks

- Take photos with your phone at each stage of disassembly; this can be invaluable for reassembly.
- Place a rag over the drain to prevent small screws or parts from falling into the pipes.
- Before installing new parts, use a wire brush to clean any mineral deposits or corrosion from the faucet body's interior.
- Keep spare washers and O-rings on hand for future minor repairs.

## Warnings

- ⚠️ Always ensure the main water supply is completely off before beginning any work on your faucet to prevent flooding.
- ⚠️ Do not overtighten screws or nuts, as this can strip threads or crack components, leading to further leaks or damage.
- ⚠️ If you encounter significant resistance when removing parts or are unsure about any step, consider consulting a professional plumber to avoid causing more extensive damage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What causes a bathtub faucet to leak?**

Most bathtub faucet leaks are caused by worn-out components inside the faucet body. This commonly includes deteriorated rubber washers, O-rings, or a faulty cartridge that has lost its sealing ability over time. Mineral deposits can also contribute to wear and tear on these parts.

**How do I know if I need a new washer or cartridge to fix my bathtub faucet?**

You'll need to disassemble the faucet to inspect the internal components. If you have compression faucets (separate hot and cold handles), you'll likely find washers. Single-handle faucets typically use cartridges. Look for visible cracks, flattening, or brittleness in the rubber washers or signs of corrosion and breakage on the cartridge.

**Is it difficult to fix a leaky bathtub faucet myself?**

For common issues like worn washers or cartridges, fixing a leaky bathtub faucet is generally considered an easy DIY project. With the right tools and a clear guide, most homeowners can complete the repair in under an hour. More complex issues might require professional help.

**What tools do I need to fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

You'll typically need a set of screwdrivers (Phillips and flathead), an adjustable wrench, and possibly pliers. A utility knife or small flathead screwdriver is useful for removing decorative caps. Don't forget replacement parts like washers, O-rings, or a new cartridge, along with plumber's grease.

**When should I consider replacing the entire faucet instead of just fixing a leak?**

If your faucet is old, severely corroded, or if replacement parts are difficult to find, it might be more cost-effective and efficient to replace the entire unit. Frequent leaks, despite repairs, or a desire for an updated look are also good reasons to consider a full replacement. For a complete guide, see [How to Change a Bathtub Faucet: A DIY Replacement Guide](/en/how-to-change-a-bathtub-faucet).

**How to fix bathtub faucet if it's still dripping after replacing parts?**

If your bathtub faucet is still dripping, double-check that all replacement parts are the correct size and type for your faucet model. Ensure all nuts and screws are tightened appropriately, but not overtightened. Sometimes, mineral deposits in the valve seat can prevent a good seal; cleaning these thoroughly with a wire brush or fine-grit sandpaper can help.
