A leaky bathtub faucet is a common household nuisance, but it's often a straightforward DIY fix. Most leaks stem from worn-out internal components like washers, O-rings, or cartridges, which can be replaced with basic tools and a bit of patience.

## What You Need

- Screwdriver set (Phillips and flathead)
- Adjustable wrench or pliers
- Utility knife or flat-blade screwdriver (for prying caps)
- Penetrating oil (optional, for stuck screws)
- Replacement washers, O-rings, or cartridge (match your faucet type)
- Plumber's grease or silicone lubricant
- Rags or towels
- Flashlight (optional)
- Small wire brush (for cleaning)

## Steps

### Step 1: Shut Off the Water Supply

Before starting any plumbing work, it's crucial to turn off the water supply to your bathtub. Locate the main shut-off valve for your house, or if available, the specific shut-off valves for the bathroom or bathtub. Turn the faucet on briefly to drain any remaining water from the lines, ensuring there's no pressure.

### Step 2: Remove Faucet Handles and Trim

Carefully pry off any decorative caps on the faucet handles, typically found on top or front. Use a screwdriver to remove the screws holding the handles in place, then pull the handles off. Next, remove any escutcheon plates or trim that cover the faucet body, often held by screws or simply threaded on.

### Step 3: Identify Faucet Type and Worn Component

Once the handles and trim are off, you'll expose the internal mechanism. Depending on your faucet type (compression, cartridge, or ball valve), you'll look for different components. Compression faucets have a stem with a washer at the bottom; cartridge faucets have a removable cartridge; and ball faucets have a rotating ball assembly. Inspect for visible wear, corrosion, or cracks on washers, O-rings, or the cartridge itself.

### Step 4: Replace the Faulty Part

For compression faucets, unscrew the stem assembly and replace the rubber washer at the bottom, often secured by a small screw. Also, replace the O-ring around the stem. For cartridge faucets, unscrew the retaining nut or clip and pull out the old cartridge; insert the new one, ensuring it's oriented correctly. For ball faucets, remove the cam, packing, and ball, replacing all O-rings and the ball itself if worn. Apply plumber's grease to new rubber components.

### Step 5: Clean and Lubricate Internal Components

Before reassembly, take time to clean any mineral deposits or corrosion from the faucet body and internal components using a small wire brush or an old toothbrush. Apply a thin layer of plumber's grease or silicone lubricant to all new rubber parts and threads. This helps prevent future leaks and ensures smooth operation of the faucet handles.

### Step 6: Reassemble the Faucet

Carefully reassemble the faucet in reverse order. Ensure all screws are tightened snugly but do not overtighten, as this can damage components. Replace the trim plates and faucet handles, making sure they are aligned properly. Double-check that all parts are securely in place before proceeding to the final step.

### Step 7: Restore Water and Test for Leaks

Slowly turn the water supply back on, listening for any unusual noises. Once the water is fully restored, thoroughly inspect the faucet for any signs of leaks, paying close attention to the base of the handles and the spout. Turn the faucet on and off several times to ensure proper function and no dripping. If a leak persists, you may need to re-evaluate the components or consider professional help.


## Tips & Tricks

- Always use genuine replacement parts or high-quality universal alternatives that precisely match your faucet's make and model. Ill-fitting parts can lead to continued leaks.
- Take photos during disassembly to help remember the order of parts for easier reassembly.
- Keep a small bucket or rag handy to catch any residual water when disassembling the faucet.
- If screws are seized, apply penetrating oil and let it sit for a few minutes before attempting to loosen them.
- Consider replacing both hot and cold side components at the same time, even if only one is leaking, as they likely have similar wear.

## Warnings

- ⚠️ Always ensure the main water supply is completely shut off before attempting any repairs to prevent flooding.
- ⚠️ Do not overtighten screws or nuts, as this can strip threads or crack plastic components, leading to more significant damage.
- ⚠️ If you encounter extreme difficulty or are unsure about any step, it's best to consult a professional plumber to avoid further complications.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How to fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

To fix a leaky bathtub faucet, you typically need to shut off the water, disassemble the faucet handles, identify the worn component (like a washer, O-ring, or cartridge), replace it with a new one, and then reassemble the faucet. Testing for leaks afterward is essential.

**What causes a bathtub faucet to leak?**

Most bathtub faucet leaks are caused by worn-out internal components. These commonly include deteriorated rubber washers, O-rings, or a faulty cartridge that no longer creates a tight seal. Mineral deposits can also build up, preventing parts from seating correctly.

**How do you fix a dripping bathtub faucet handle?**

A dripping bathtub faucet handle usually indicates a problem with the compression washer or cartridge inside that specific handle. You'll need to turn off the water, remove the handle, and replace the worn washer or cartridge responsible for sealing that side of the faucet.

**How to fix bathtub faucet that won't stop dripping?**

If your bathtub faucet won't stop dripping, the most common fix involves replacing the internal sealing components. This usually means replacing the worn-out rubber washers, O-rings, or the entire cartridge, depending on the type of faucet you have.

**What tools are essential for fixing a leaky bathtub faucet?**

Essential tools for fixing a leaky bathtub faucet include screwdrivers (Phillips and flathead), an adjustable wrench or pliers, a utility knife for prying, and the correct replacement parts (washers, O-rings, or a cartridge). Plumber's grease is also very helpful.

**When should I consider replacing the entire faucet instead of repairing it?**

You should consider replacing the entire faucet if the body is corroded, parts are impossible to find, or if the repair costs approach the price of a new unit. Persistent leaks after multiple repairs or significant aesthetic damage are also good reasons to consider a full replacement. For guidance, see [How to Change a Bathtub Faucet: A DIY Guide](/en/how-to-change-a-bathtub-faucet-diy).

**Can I fix a leaky bathtub faucet myself, or do I need a plumber?**

Many leaky bathtub faucets can be fixed by a DIY enthusiast with basic tools and knowledge. However, if you're uncomfortable with plumbing, encounter seized parts, or can't identify the source of the leak, it's best to call a professional plumber.
