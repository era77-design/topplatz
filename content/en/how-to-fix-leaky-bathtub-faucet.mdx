A persistent drip from your bathtub faucet is not only annoying but also wastes water and can lead to higher utility bills. Often, the culprit is a worn-out internal component like a washer, O-ring, or cartridge. Fixing a leaky bathtub faucet is a common DIY project that requires basic tools and a little patience.

## What You Need

- Screwdriver set (Phillips and flathead)
- Adjustable wrench
- Utility knife or small flathead screwdriver
- Replacement washers, O-rings, or a new cartridge (match existing parts)
- Plumber's grease (silicone-based)
- Clean rags
- Bucket or small container
- Penetrating oil (optional, for stubborn screws)

## Steps

### Step 1: Turn Off the Water Supply

Before starting any work, locate the main water shut-off valve for your house or, if available, the shut-off valves for your bathroom. Turn them off completely to prevent water flow. Open the faucet to drain any remaining water from the pipes, ensuring no unexpected sprays while you work.

### Step 2: Cover the Drain and Remove Handles

Place a rag or stopper over the bathtub drain to prevent small screws or parts from falling into it. Carefully remove the decorative caps (if any) from the faucet handles, usually by prying them off with a small flathead screwdriver. Unscrew the handle screws and pull the handles off to expose the valve stems or cartridge nuts.

> 💡 **Tip:** Take a photo of the faucet assembly before you start disassembling it. This will be a helpful reference during reassembly.

### Step 3: Remove the Faucet Stems or Cartridge

Depending on your faucet type (compression, disc, ball, or cartridge), you will either see hexagonal nuts securing valve stems or a cartridge held in place by a retaining nut or clip. Use an adjustable wrench to loosen and remove the nuts, then carefully pull out the stems or cartridge. You may need to use a utility knife to pry off older, stubborn parts.

> 💡 **Tip:** If parts are stuck due to mineral buildup, apply penetrating oil and let it sit for a few minutes before trying again.

### Step 4: Identify and Replace Worn Components

Inspect the removed stems or cartridge for obvious signs of wear, such as cracked washers, brittle O-rings, or a corroded cartridge. These are the most common causes of leaks. Replace any damaged washers or O-rings with new ones of the exact same size. If you have a cartridge faucet, replace the entire cartridge unit with a new matching one.

> 💡 **Tip:** Take the old parts to a hardware store to ensure you purchase exact replacements. This prevents compatibility issues.

### Step 5: Apply Plumber's Grease and Reassemble

Apply a thin layer of plumber's grease to new O-rings and threaded parts to ensure smooth operation and a good seal. Carefully reinsert the new components into the faucet body. Tighten the retaining nuts or secure the clips, then reattach the faucet handles and decorative caps. Do not overtighten, as this can damage the new parts or threads.

> 💡 **Tip:** Hand-tighten first, then use a wrench for a final snug turn, being mindful not to strip threads.

### Step 6: Restore Water Supply and Test for Leaks

Slowly turn the main water supply back on. Check the faucet for any immediate leaks around the handles or spout. Turn the faucet on and off several times, checking for drips and ensuring the handles operate smoothly. If a drip persists, you may need to re-examine your work or consider professional help.

> 💡 **Tip:** Run the water for a minute to flush out any air in the lines after reassembling.


## Tips & Tricks

- Always turn off the main water supply before attempting any plumbing repairs to prevent accidental flooding.
- Keep a small container or bucket handy to catch any residual water when disassembling the faucet.
- If you cannot find the exact replacement parts, taking the old ones to a specialty plumbing supply store can often yield better results than a general hardware store.

## Warnings

- ⚠️ Ensure the main water supply is completely off before starting to avoid severe water damage.
- ⚠️ Avoid using excessive force when tightening components, as this can strip threads or crack plastic parts, leading to more significant problems.
- ⚠️ If you are unsure about any step or the leak persists after your repair attempts, it's best to consult a professional plumber.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do you fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

Fixing a leaky bathtub faucet typically involves turning off the water, disassembling the faucet handles, removing the valve stems or cartridge, and replacing worn-out components like washers, O-rings, or the entire cartridge. Reassemble the faucet, turn the water back on, and test for leaks.

**What are common reasons I need to fix my bathtub faucet?**

The most common reasons for a leaky bathtub faucet are worn-out rubber washers, deteriorated O-rings, or a faulty cartridge. Mineral buildup can also prevent parts from sealing properly, leading to drips and leaks.

**What tools do I need to fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

You'll generally need a screwdriver set, an adjustable wrench, a utility knife, replacement parts (washers, O-rings, or cartridge), and plumber's grease. A bucket and rags are also useful for containing water.

**How do I fix my bathtub faucet if it's a single-handle type?**

For a single-handle bathtub faucet, the process is similar but focuses on replacing the internal cartridge. After turning off the water and removing the handle, you'll typically remove a retaining clip or nut to pull out and replace the entire cartridge unit.

**How much does it cost to fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

The cost to fix a leaky bathtub faucet yourself is usually minimal, often just the price of replacement washers, O-rings, or a cartridge, which can range from a few dollars to $30-$50. Hiring a plumber can cost significantly more, depending on labor rates and parts.

**When should I consider replacing my bathtub faucet instead of fixing it?**

Consider replacing your bathtub faucet if it's very old, heavily corroded, parts are difficult to find, or if the leak persists after multiple repair attempts. Sometimes, the cost of persistent repairs can outweigh the cost of a new faucet.
