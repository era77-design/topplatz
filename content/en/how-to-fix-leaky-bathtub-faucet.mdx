A leaky bathtub faucet is usually caused by worn-out washers, O-rings, or a faulty cartridge inside the handle assembly. Fixing it is a common DIY task that can save water, prevent damage, and eliminate annoying drips. This guide will walk you through the steps to quickly resolve most bathtub faucet leaks.

## What You Need

- Screwdriver (Phillips and/or flathead)
- Adjustable wrench or basin wrench
- Utility knife or small flathead screwdriver (for prying caps)
- Replacement washers, O-rings, or cartridge (specific to your faucet type)
- Plumber's grease (silicone-based)
- Rag or towel
- Penetrating oil (optional, for stuck parts)
- Safety glasses (optional)

## Steps

### Step 1: Turn Off the Water Supply

Locate the main water shut-off valve for your home or the specific shut-off valves for the bathroom. Turn them off completely to prevent flooding. Turn on the faucet to drain any remaining water from the lines, ensuring the pressure is relieved.

### Step 2: Identify Faucet Type and Disassemble Handle

Bathtub faucets typically use either compression valves or cartridge valves. Carefully pry off any decorative caps on the handles (usually with a utility knife or small screwdriver) to expose the screws. Unscrew and remove the handles, then remove any escutcheon plates or trim that cover the valve body.

### Step 3: Remove the Faucet Stem or Cartridge

For compression faucets, use an adjustable wrench to unscrew the packing nut and then the valve stem. For cartridge faucets, there might be a retaining clip or nut holding the cartridge in place; remove it according to your faucet model's instructions, then pull out the cartridge. Take note of its orientation for reinstallation.

### Step 4: Inspect and Replace Worn Parts

Examine the removed stem or cartridge. For compression faucets, replace the rubber washer at the end of the stem and the O-rings or packing washer around the stem. For cartridge faucets, replace the entire cartridge with a new one that matches your old one exactly. Apply a thin layer of plumber's grease to new O-rings or cartridges for easier installation and better sealing.

### Step 5: Reassemble the Faucet

Carefully reinsert the new stem or cartridge into the faucet body, ensuring it's properly aligned. Tighten the packing nut or secure the retaining clip/nut, being careful not to overtighten. Reattach the escutcheon plates, handles, and decorative caps in reverse order of disassembly. Ensure all parts are snug but not forced.

### Step 6: Restore Water Supply and Test

Slowly turn the main water supply back on. Check for any immediate leaks around the faucet handles or spout. Turn the faucet on and off several times to ensure it's functioning correctly and that the leak has been stopped. Look for any drips from the spout or base, and confirm smooth operation of the handles.


## Tips & Tricks

- Take a photo of your faucet and its disassembled parts before you start; this can be helpful for reassembly and when buying replacement parts.
- Bring the old washer, O-ring, or cartridge to the hardware store to ensure you get an exact match for your faucet model.
- If parts are stuck, a penetrating oil can help loosen them, but be careful not to damage plastic components.
- Regularly clean mineral deposits from your faucet components to prevent future leaks and ensure smooth operation.
- Keep a spare set of common washers and O-rings on hand if you know your faucet type to quickly address future minor leaks.

## Warnings

- ⚠️ Always turn off the main water supply before attempting any plumbing repairs to avoid flooding and potential water damage.
- ⚠️ Do not overtighten screws or nuts, as this can strip threads, crack plastic components, or damage the faucet body, leading to more significant problems.
- ⚠️ Be cautious when using tools; wear safety glasses to protect your eyes from flying debris or splashing water.
- ⚠️ If you encounter excessive corrosion, stripped screws, or if the leak persists after replacing parts, it may be time to consult a professional plumber or consider replacing the entire faucet.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I fix a leaky bathtub faucet?**

To fix a leaky bathtub faucet, first turn off the water supply. Then, disassemble the handle to access and replace worn-out washers, O-rings, or the entire cartridge, depending on your faucet type. Reassemble and test the faucet for drips.

**What causes a bathtub faucet to leak?**

Most bathtub faucet leaks are caused by degraded rubber washers or O-rings inside compression faucets, or a faulty cartridge in newer single-handle faucets. Mineral buildup from hard water can also accelerate wear on these internal components.

**What tools do I need to repair a leaky bathtub faucet?**

You'll typically need a screwdriver (Phillips and/or flathead), an adjustable wrench, replacement washers/O-rings/cartridge specific to your faucet, and possibly plumber's grease. A utility knife can also help remove decorative caps.

**Can I fix a leaky bathtub faucet myself, or do I need a plumber?**

Many common bathtub faucet leaks can be fixed by a DIY enthusiast with basic tools and knowledge. However, if you encounter severe corrosion, stripped parts, or if the leak persists after attempting repairs, it's best to call a professional plumber.

**How do I know if I have a compression or cartridge faucet?**

Compression faucets typically have two separate handles for hot and cold water that you turn multiple times to open and close. Cartridge faucets often have a single handle that you lift and turn, or two handles that turn only a quarter or half-turn.

**What if replacing the washer or cartridge doesn't stop the leak?**

If the leak persists after replacing the appropriate internal components, the faucet body itself might be cracked, severely corroded, or there could be an issue with the valve seat. In such cases, you might need to replace the entire faucet. For guidance on this, see [How to Change a Bathtub Faucet: A DIY Guide](/en/how-to-change-a-bathtub-faucet).

**How often should I perform maintenance on my bathtub faucet?**

Proactive maintenance isn't typically scheduled, but it's a good practice to address leaks promptly as they occur. If you notice reduced water pressure or stiffness in the handles, it might indicate wear that could lead to future leaks, prompting an inspection.
